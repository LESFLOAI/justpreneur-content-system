## Approved Facts Block (verbatim, in use)
On October 28, 2026, DoorDash, Inc. (NASDAQ: DASH) and Maplebear Inc. d/b/a Instacart (NASDAQ: CART) signed a definitive merger agreement under which DoorDash will acquire Instacart for $65.00 per share — an equity value of approximately $17 billion — representing a reported 26% premium to Instacart's October 22, 2026 closing price. DoorDash filed an 8-K disclosing the agreement on October 29, 2026. Instacart shareholders may elect to receive $65.00 in cash, 0.3372 shares of DoorDash Class A common stock, or a mixed election of $48.75 cash plus 0.0843 DoorDash shares, subject to proration so that stock consideration does not exceed 25% of total deal consideration (at least 75% cash). Instacart's board has unanimously approved the deal, and holders of roughly 19% of Instacart shares have agreed to vote in favor. The companies state the deal is expected to close by the end of 2027 or in the first half of 2028, subject to Instacart stockholder approval, expiration of the U.S. Hart-Scott-Rodino antitrust waiting period, and competition approvals in Canada and Israel; the agreement includes a $650 million termination fee payable by Instacart under specified circumstances. DoorDash and Instacart state they estimate combining DashPass and Instacart+ into a single membership would save combined members approximately $100 million per year in membership fees — an estimate from the companies, not an independently verified figure. Instacart's current CEO is Chris Rogers (since August 2025); prior CEO Fidji Simo departed to lead an applications/consumer business role at OpenAI and should not be referenced as Instacart's current CEO. According to Reuters and Wall Street Journal reporting citing people familiar with the matter, Uber had earlier held preliminary acquisition talks with Instacart that were reported as no longer active by around October 26, 2026 — this has not been confirmed on record by Uber or Instacart. On October 26, 2026 — the day WSJ/Reuters first reported DoorDash was in "advanced talks" to acquire Instacart — Instacart shares closed up approximately 7.9% and DoorDash shares closed down approximately 6.1%; these moves reflect the market's reaction to the talks reports, not to the October 28-29 signed-deal announcement. As of October 30, 2026, no termination, regulatory block, lawsuit, or renegotiation of the agreement has been reported; the deal remains pending and subject to a lengthy regulatory and shareholder-approval process.

## Angle 1 — "The Fine Print Is the Deal" (Deal Architecture / Election Mechanics)

1. **Thesis:** The real entrepreneurial lesson in the DoorDash-Instacart merger isn't the $17B headline — it's the cash/stock election structure with a 25% stock cap, which shows how negotiated terms, not the top-line number, determine who actually carries risk after signing.
2. **Entrepreneurial lesson:** Ownership and execution — when structuring any deal (exit, raise, partnership), the mechanism of payment (cash vs. equity, proration caps, timing) matters more than the headline valuation. Builders should learn to read deal structure, not just the number in the press release.
3. **Hook:** "DoorDash is paying $17 billion for Instacart. The real story is in three sentences buried in the 8-K."
4. **Audience benefit:** Teaches operators and founders to evaluate future exit, investment, or partnership offers beyond the sticker price — understanding cash/stock mix, proration, and what it signals about a buyer's confidence and a seller's risk tolerance.
5. **Best format:** Carousel — this is a structural, explainer-type concept best taught visually with a clean breakdown of the three election paths.
6. **Length:** 7 slides (hook, headline number, the three election options, why proration caps stock at 25%, what that signals about buyer cash position, the lesson for operators, CTA).
7. **Visual concept:** Three parallel "payout lanes" ($65 cash / 0.3372 shares / mixed $48.75 + 0.0843 shares) stacked above a "75% cash floor" bar, clean financial-infographic style.
8. **Risk of misunderstanding:** Could read as investment/trading advice on share elections; must stay framed as deal-structure literacy for founders, not guidance on how Instacart shareholders should vote or trade.

## Angle 2 — "The Rumor Already Priced It" (Narrative Timing / Information Control)

1. **Thesis:** The market finished reacting to the DoorDash-Instacart deal two days before it was even signed — proof that in today's information environment, the value-moving event is the leak, not the announcement.
2. **Entrepreneurial lesson:** Timing and visibility — builders who wait for the "official" moment to tell their story are often already late. Investors, customers, and audiences form their verdict from signals and anticipation before the formal reveal. Controlling the narrative before the official moment is the real leverage point.
3. **Hook:** "By the time DoorDash and Instacart signed anything, the market had already made up its mind — two days earlier."
4. **Audience benefit:** Helps founders and creators understand that pre-announcement narrative (signals, teasers, anticipation) often carries more weight than the final reveal — and that launch strategy should be designed around that reality instead of treating "announcement day" as the main event.
5. **Best format:** Reel — this is a sequential, before/after story best told fast with a timeline narration.
6. **Length:** 45–60 seconds.
7. **Visual concept:** Split-screen stock-chart animation — Oct 26 ("talks" report: Instacart up ~7.9%, DoorDash down ~6.1%) vs. Oct 28-29 (signed deal: comparatively flat), with an on-screen timestamp ticker.
8. **Risk of misunderstanding:** Must not imply insider trading, leaking, or wrongdoing by any party — keep language tied to "reported talks" and attributed reporting (WSJ/Reuters), and clearly label the Oct 26 move as a reaction to talks coverage, not the signed deal, per the facts block.

## Angle 3 — "Showing Up Second Didn't Cost DoorDash the Deal" (Execution Speed vs. Early Mover)

1. **Thesis:** Uber reportedly had the earlier conversation with Instacart, but DoorDash is the one with a signed, board-approved agreement — proof that in competitive deal-making, closing beats being first.
2. **Entrepreneurial lesson:** Execution and leverage — being early to a conversation means nothing without the follow-through to signature. Founders should spend energy converting interest into committed terms rather than racing to be the first name mentioned.
3. **Hook:** "Uber talked to Instacart first. DoorDash is the one that signed."
4. **Audience benefit:** Reinforces to founders and operators chasing partnerships, capital, or acquirers that momentum and follow-through — not being first in the room — determines outcomes. Useful reframe for anyone mid-negotiation who feels behind.
5. **Best format:** Short carousel (simple contrast doesn't need much runway, but a few slides let the attribution caveat breathe).
6. **Length:** 4–5 slides (hook, Uber's earlier reported talks — attributed to anonymous sourcing, DoorDash's signed/board-approved deal, the lesson, CTA).
7. **Visual concept:** Two-lane "race" graphic — one lane stalled/exited (Uber, labeled "reported talks, no longer active"), the other crossing a finish line labeled "signed agreement" (DoorDash).
8. **Risk of misunderstanding:** Must keep Uber's involvement framed as unconfirmed, anonymously sourced reporting — not stated as fact by Uber or Instacart. Risk of the post reading as mocking Uber; tone must stay strategic and neutral, not triumphant or dismissive.
